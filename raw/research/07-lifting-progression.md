# Progressing strength training alongside endurance running

Question: How should a 30–40 km/week runner lifting twice a week build progressive overload into heavy strength, calf and plyometric work without harming running or a recently settled Achilles?

## Findings

1. **Heavy strength training improves running economy by roughly 2–8% in trained runners, with little effect on VO2max or body mass.**
Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes, 2018, Sports Medicine 48:1117–1149. Systematic review, 24 studies (PEDro 4–6). Running economy improved 2–8% versus running-only controls, though not in every study; time-trial and sprint performance tended to improve; VO2max, vVO2max, lactate and body composition typically unaffected. Practical summary suggests 2–3 sessions/week of mixed modalities (secondary summary, not checked against full text). Abstract only.
https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/33750

2. **High-load and combined (heavy + plyometric) methods work; submaximal and isometric methods show no clear running economy benefit.**
Llanos-Lagos, Ramirez-Campillo, Moran & Sáez de Villarreal, 2024, Sports Medicine 54:895–932. Systematic review and meta-analysis. Running economy effect sizes: high load ES −0.27 (small), plyometric ES −0.31 at ≤12 km/h (small), combined ES −0.43 (moderate); submaximal and isometric not significant (p > 0.13). Effects larger at higher speeds and higher VO2max. GRADE certainty moderate (low for combined). Abstract/secondary summary only.
https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/

3. **Heavy strength plus plyometrics twice a week for 10 weeks made running economy more durable late in a long run.**
Zanini et al., 2025, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 57(7):1546–1558. RCT, n = 28 well-trained men (14 run-only control). Running economy at 90 min of heavy-domain running: −2.1% (better) vs +0.6% in controls (p = 0.04). Time to exhaustion at 95% VO2max after the 90-min run: +35% vs −8% (p = 0.004). Exact set/rep/load prescription not seen (abstract only; PDF unreadable).
https://oro.open.ac.uk/107447

4. **Low-rep near-maximal squats (4×4RM) gave large strength gains and ~5% better economy without weight gain.**
Støren, Helgerud, Støa & Hoff, 2008, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise. RCT, n = 17 well-trained runners. Half-squat 4×4RM, 3×/week, 8 weeks: 1RM +33%, rate of force development +26%, running economy +5%, time to exhaustion at maximal aerobic speed +21%; no change in VO2max or body mass. A secondary source says load rose 2.5 kg when >4 reps were achieved (not confirmed from abstract). Abstract only.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18460997/

5. **A long periodised programme (2×/week then 1×/week) kept improving economy and strength in competitive runners over 40 weeks.**
Beattie, Carson, Lyons, Rossiter & Kenny, 2017, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research 31(1):9–23. RCT, n = 20 (11 strength, 9 control). 20 weeks maximal and reactive strength 2×/week, then 20 weeks 1×/week. Running economy, vVO2max, maximal and reactive strength improved at weeks 20 and 40; no body composition change. Abstract only.
https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-effect-of-strength-training-on-performance-indicators-in-dist/

6. **Once a strength base is built, one heavy session a week maintained strength and muscle size over a 13-week season (cyclists).**
Rønnestad, Hansen & Raastad, 2010, European Journal of Applied Physiology. Controlled trial in well-trained cyclists: 12 weeks heavy strength 2×/week, then 1×/week maintenance for 13 weeks; gains in strength and cross-sectional area preserved. Cyclists, not runners. Abstract only.
https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/170841
Related: Basel *Sports* 2020 study, n = 8 middle-distance runners, running economy gains from explosive strength persisted through 4 weeks of stopping strength work (very small sample).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7821917

7. **Concurrent training does not meaningfully blunt maximal strength or hypertrophy, but does blunt explosive strength, especially when done in the same session.**
Schumann et al., 2022, Sports Medicine 52:601–612. Meta-analysis, 43 studies. Maximal strength SMD −0.06, hypertrophy −0.01 (no interference); explosive strength SMD −0.28 (95% CI −0.48 to −0.08), worse when in the same session. Independent of aerobic modality, frequency, training status. Abstract only.
https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf
Older contrast: Wilson et al., 2012, JSCR, meta-analysis of 21 studies, found interference rose with endurance frequency and duration (r ≈ −0.26 to −0.75) and with running more than cycling; the running effect depended on one outlier study.
https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/sms/resource/pt/mdl-22002517

8. **If lifting and running share a session, lift first for lower-body strength; order does not affect aerobic gains.**
Eddens, van Someren & Howatson, 2018, Sports Medicine 48:177–188. Meta-analysis, 10 studies (≥5 weeks). Resistance-then-endurance gave ~6.9% greater lower-body dynamic strength (95% CI 2.0–11.9%); no order effect on hypertrophy, static strength or VO2max. A separate 2018 J Sports Sci meta-analysis: +3.96 kg lower-body 1RM when strength came first. Abstract only.
https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/31702

9. **Heavy lower-body lifting can impair running economy and hard-running performance for up to a day or more; allow ~24 h before quality runs.**
Doma et al., 2017, Sports Medicine (review). Resistance-training stress can persist for days; running economy impaired for several days after a single bout in some studies; time to exhaustion reduced for up to 24 h. Authors decline to give a fixed recovery time. The 3–6 h same-day gap and "24 h is best" are practitioner guidance, not trial data. Abstract/secondary summary only.
https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-017-0758-3

10. **Undulating periodisation slightly outperforms linear for 1RM in trained people, but the effect is small and uncertain.**
Moesgaard et al., 2022, Sports Medicine 52(7):1647–1666. Meta-analysis. Periodised vs non-periodised ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–0.57); undulating vs linear ES 0.31 (0.02–0.61), benefit only in trained participants; no hypertrophy difference. Harries et al., 2015, meta-analysis, found no linear vs undulating difference. Abstract only. No study found that tests models specifically in concurrent endurance athletes.
https://biomed.reviewer.ly/app/publication/9b9c75c4-fcd5-45af-9e7e-8c23e84d9f8a

11. **RPE-based load selection works at least as well as percentage-based loading; autoregulation is not clearly superior.**
Helms et al., 2018, Frontiers in Physiology. RCT, n = 21 trained men, 8 weeks, squat and bench 3×/week undulating. Both groups gained; no significant difference; small probable advantage for RPE group in squat (79% chance). Full text open, not read.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/
Hickmott et al., 2022, Sports Medicine – Open. Meta-analysis: autoregulated vs percentage loading, 1RM difference 2.07 kg (95% CI −0.32 to 4.46; p = 0.09), i.e. similar. Velocity-loss thresholds ≤25% gave more strength than >25%.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/

12. **RIR estimates are more accurate in low-rep sets and closer to failure; less accurate when far from failure.**
Zourdos et al., 2016, JSCR, n = 29 (15 experienced, 14 novice squatters): introduced RIR-based RPE; RPE related inversely to bar velocity. A 2019 follow-up (n = 25, squat to failure at 70% 1RM): ratings more accurate near failure; more reps per set predicted larger error at RPE 5 and 7; training age did not predict accuracy. Abstracts only.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747900/

13. **Strength gains are not much affected by how close to failure sets are taken (unlike hypertrophy).**
Robinson, Pelland, Refalo, Zourdos et al., 2024, Sports Medicine. Meta-regression: negligible relationship between proximity to failure and strength gain; hypertrophy benefits more from proximity. Press release suggests stopping ~3–5 reps short for strength (practitioner reading). Secondary summaries only.
https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study

14. **Load increments: practitioner convention is the "2-for-2" rule with ~5–10% (lower body) and ~2–5% (upper body) jumps.**
Practitioner/textbook convention (NSCA, ACSM study materials; not trial data). Add load when the lifter beats the rep target by ≥2 on the last set in two consecutive sessions. Lower body ~5–10% or ~2.5–5 kg; upper body ~2–5% or ~1–2.5 kg.
https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/nsca-cpt/acute-variables/progression-reassessment

15. **Achilles: continued loading under a pain-monitoring model is as good as rest; heavy slow resistance is as effective as eccentrics with better adherence.**
Silbernagel et al., 2007, American Journal of Sports Medicine 35(6):897–906. RCT, n = 38: continuing running/jumping under pain monitoring vs 6 weeks' rest from them; no difference in outcomes, both improved to 12 months. Summaries disagree on whether the threshold was <5 or ≤5/10. Abstract only.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17307888/
Beyer et al., 2015, AJSM 43(7):1704–1711. RCT, n = 58: heavy slow resistance vs eccentric for 12 weeks; equal VISA-A gains held at 52 weeks; adherence 92% vs 78%. Abstract only.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018970/

16. **A criteria-based Achilles protocol gates plyometrics on calf strength relative to bodyweight and hop pain, then progresses pogo hops → single-leg hops → drop jumps.**
Griffin, Daniels, Hill, Franklyn-Miller & Morin, 2021, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders 22:695. RCT protocol (no results), planned n = 60. Full text read. Level 4 (wk 6–9): entry pain <5/10 on 10 double-leg hops; seated calf raise aim >110% BW, standing single-leg >80% BW; double-leg pogo 4×10. Level 5 (wk 9–12): entry pain <4/10 on 10 single-leg hops; single-leg pogo 4×10, drop jumps 4×4 from 20–30 cm; standing single-leg calf >100% BW. Level 6 (maintenance, to 26 wk): drop jumps 4×5, single-leg pogo 4×12, isometric target >140% BW. Load rises weekly only if pain does not rise; if pain >5/10 next day, add a recovery day. Proposed targets, not validated thresholds.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8364697

17. **No good evidence that upper-body strength improves distance running; no controlled study found on pull-up progression methods.**
Searches found only coaching content and indirect arm-swing studies. Negatives, sub-maximal sets and frequent practice ("greasing the groove") are practitioner methods with no trial comparing them.

## Grades

| Conclusion | Grade |
|---|---|
| Heavy strength (and heavy + plyometric) improves running economy 2–8% in trained runners | W |
| Combined heavy + plyometric is at least as good as heavy alone; light/isometric work is not enough for economy | I |
| Strength helps economy durability late in long efforts (relevant to backyard ultra) | I (one RCT) |
| Concurrent running at this volume does not meaningfully blunt maximal strength; explosive strength more vulnerable | W |
| Lift before running if same session; ~24 h before hard runs | I (order) / H (24 h gap) |
| Once built, strength can be maintained on ~1 heavy session/week | I (cyclists; runners indirect) |
| No progression model clearly best; undulating marginally ahead in trained lifters | I |
| RPE/RIR loading works as well as %1RM; RIR accurate within ~1–2 reps on low-rep sets | I |
| Strength gains do not need sets near failure (RPE 7–8 is enough) | I |
| 2-for-2 rule, ~2.5–5 kg lower-body jumps | H |
| Pain-monitored continued loading and heavy slow calf work for Achilles | W |
| Bodyweight-ratio gates (seated ≥110% BW, hop pain <5/10) before plyometrics | H (unvalidated protocol) |
| Upper-body strength benefits running; pull-up progression methods | H |

## Gaps and caveats

- Almost all sources read as abstracts or secondary summaries; exact prescriptions (sets, %1RM, progression rules) of Zanini, Beattie and Rønnestad not verified.
- Trial populations are mostly well-trained young men over 8–40 weeks; none are 24-hour ultra runners. Durability evidence rests on one 90-minute RCT.
- No study tests progression models (linear vs double vs RPE) inside a concurrent endurance programme at 2×/week.
- Recovery-gap evidence is acute and inconsistent; the 24 h figure is a convention.
- The Achilles strength gates are from a trial protocol, not results; no validated return-to-plyometrics threshold found. Silbernagel & Crossley 2015 (JOSPT 45(11):876–886) holds return-to-sport criteria but its text was not seen.
- Whether loads must keep rising indefinitely is not answered; the evidence suggests economy gains can hold on maintenance dosing, but no runner trial tests "maintain at threshold" vs "keep progressing".
- Not medical advice.

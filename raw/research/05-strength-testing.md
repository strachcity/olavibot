# Strength testing — true 1RM vs multi-rep and estimated 1RM

Question: for a recreational endurance athlete who has never maxed, should end-of-block squat and deadlift testing be a true 1RM, a 3RM, or an estimate from submaximal sets?

## Findings

1. **A true 1RM is highly reliable, even without familiarisation, but a first-ever test tends to under-read.**
   Grgic, Lazinica, Schoenfeld & Pedisic, 2020, *Sports Medicine – Open* 6:31. Systematic review, 32 studies, pooled n = 1,595. Full text read.
   Median ICC 0.97 (range 0.64–0.99), median CV 4.2%. With no familiarisation, median ICC was 0.96 and CV 5.3%; with one or more sessions, 0.98 and 3.8%. Two-thirds of analyses found no systematic change between test and retest. Where there was a change, the retest was higher: a median of 5.5 kg for lower-body lifts (range 1.1–17.5 kg). The authors judged familiarisation unnecessary in general but useful where technique is shaky. The review contained no deadlift studies. The free-weight back squat was reliable (ICC 0.97–0.99), but one study gave a squat intra-rater ICC of only 0.64, which the authors put down to technical complexity.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7367986/

2. **Lifters without experience keep "gaining" across repeat 1RM tests; trained lifters stabilise quickly.**
   Ritti-Dias et al., 2011, *J Strength Cond Res* 25(5):1418–22. Repeated-measures design: four 1RM sessions on separate days, bench press and squat; n not confirmed. Abstract only.
   Only the group without training experience improved across sessions. Their squat was +7.6%, +10.1% and +11.2% on session 1 by sessions 2, 3 and 4. Related work (seen only as secondary citations): Ribeiro et al. 2014 found untrained men stabilised by sessions 2–3. Soares-Caldeira et al. 2009 recommended at least two familiarisation sessions for women with prior experience.
   https://peeref.com/works/12605241

3. **For the squat, Epley or Brzycki applied to a 3–5RM predicted 1RM to within about 3 kg in trained college athletes.**
   DiStasio, 2014. Validation of Epley and Brzycki for the back squat in Division I American football players. n not confirmed. Seen only via secondary citations; the original was not retrieved.
   Epley from a 5RM came within about 2.7 kg of the measured 1RM, and Brzycki from a 3RM within about 3.1 kg. Neither differed significantly from the measured 1RM.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9465738

4. **Equations generalise poorly across lifts and systematically under-predict the deadlift.**
   LeSuer, McCormick, Mayhew et al., 1997, *J Strength Cond Res* 11:211–13. Seven equations tested in untrained college students across bench, squat and deadlift; n = 67 per a secondary source. Seen only via secondary citation (Eston & Evans 2009, full text read).
   Correlations were high (r ≈ 0.95), but only Wathan's equation predicted the squat without significant error. Every equation significantly under-predicted the deadlift 1RM, by 22–34 lb (9–14%).
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3761544

5. **Fewer reps means a more accurate prediction. Keep it to ≤10 reps, ideally ~5.**
   Reynolds, Gordon & Robergs, 2006, *J Strength Cond Res* 20(3):584–92. Prediction study, n = 70 (34 men, 36 women, aged 18–69), with 1, 5, 10 and 20RM tested on leg press and chest press. Abstract only; full PDF exists but was not read.
   The 5RM predicted best: leg press R² was 0.974 from the 5RM, 0.933 from the 10RM and 0.915 from the 20RM. Even so, the leg-press standard error from the 5RM was about 16 kg. The authors advise against linear equations above 10 reps. Dohoney et al. 2002 (cited in Eston & Evans) likewise found a 4–6RM more accurate than a 7–10RM.
   https://www.unm.edu/~rrobergs/478RMStrengthPrediction.pdf

6. **Rep-in-reserve (RIR) estimates run about one rep out on average, and are better close to failure and in low-rep sets.**
   Halperin et al., 2022, *Sports Medicine*. Meta-analysis, 12 studies, n = 414. Abstract and preprint only.
   Lifters under-predicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17–1.73), with very high heterogeneity (I² ≈ 98%). Accuracy improved slightly closer to failure and in sets of ≤12 reps. Training status did not moderate accuracy.
   https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67
   Zourdos et al., 2016, *J Strength Cond Res*. Cross-sectional, n = 29 (15 experienced, 14 novice squatters). Abstract only. RPE tracked bar velocity (r = −0.88 in experienced lifters, −0.77 in novices). Experienced lifters rated their 1RM higher (closer to 10) than novices did, so novices may under-rate a true max. A later study by the same group (n = 25, abstract only) found RIR error of about 2 reps at RPE 9, larger in longer sets.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747900/

7. **Velocity-based 1RM prediction overestimates, and the minimum-velocity method is not recommended for the free-weight squat.**
   Greig et al., 2023, *Sports Medicine*. Systematic review and individual-participant-data meta-analysis covering squat, bench, deadlift and the Olympic lifts. Abstract only.
   Predictions overestimated the 1RM by 4.5 kg on average (95% CI 1.5–7.4 kg), about 3.7%, whichever model was used. The authors recommend direct 1RM testing where feasible and see velocity estimates as useful for tracking trends. A separate 2024 back-squat meta-analysis (*J Strength Cond Res*, abstract only) concluded that the minimum-velocity-threshold method is not viable for the free-weight back squat.
   https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10432349/

8. **1RM testing has a low injury rate in the research literature, but the highest rate is in untrained, inexperienced people.**
   Grgic et al. 2020 (as in finding 1): adverse events were rare across 32 studies. Soreness was the most common; there were two aggravated back injuries and one case of quadriceps pain.
   McCully et al., older adults with and without lifting experience, n = 83. Abstract only. 2 of 83 were injured (2.4%); the rate in the untrained subgroup was about 8% per a secondary summary.
   Aging Clin Exp Res review, 2014 (abstract only): the injury risk of 1RM testing may be higher than that of training, and extra precautions are needed for inexperienced lifters.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24158788
   McCurdy et al., 2004, *J Sports Sci Med*. Unilateral squat, n = 52 (trained and untrained). Abstract only. Both the 1RM and 3RM were reliable (r 0.97–0.99 for 1RM, 0.87–0.97 for 3RM) and described as safe; a practice session before baseline was advised.
   https://www.jssm.org/jssm-03-190.xml-abst
   Context: Keogh & Winwood, 2017, *Sports Medicine* (systematic review, abstract only) report about 2–4 injuries per 1,000 h across weight-training sports, low relative to team sports. In powerlifting, the lower back and knee are among the main sites.
   https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-epidemiology-of-injuries-across-the-weight-training-sports/

9. **The standard protocol reaches the max in 3–5 attempts after a graded warm-up.**
   NSCA-derived guidance, seen only in secondary and exam-prep sources, not NSCA primary text: 5–10 reps at about 40–60% of estimated 1RM, then sets of 3–5 and 2–3 reps at rising loads, then single attempts. Lower-body jumps are about 10–20% early, getting smaller near the max. Rest is 2–4 min between attempts (often 3–5 min is cited). The aim is the 1RM within 3–5 attempts, because more attempts let fatigue suppress the result. NSW ACI clinical guidance gives a similar structure, with pre-screening.
   https://www.hprc-online.org/articles/one-rep-max-for-strength
   https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/739923/ACI-Guide-performing-1-repetition-maximum-strength-assessment.pdf

10. **Heavy lower-body lifting impairs running economy and endurance performance for about 24–48 h.**
    Doma et al., 2019, *Sports Medicine – Open* 5:21. Repeated-measures design, n = 10 men untrained in resistance work, single 6RM-based lower-body session. Full text read.
    Running energy cost was raised for up to 48 h: 1.13 kcal/kg/km at baseline, 1.19 at 24 h and 1.16 at 48 h. VO2 and ventilation were raised at 24 h. The authors advise caution with endurance sessions for at least 48 h. There was no control condition.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6548784
    Doma, Deakin & Bentley, 2017, *Sports Medicine*. Narrative review, abstract and secondary citations only. Time to exhaustion was reduced for up to 24 h after a 6RM lower-body session in endurance-trained runners. Running economy was unchanged at 6–8 h in strength-trained people, so training status matters.
    https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-017-0758-3.pdf
    A PeerJ study (abstract only) found recovery took 24 h after a heavy-load session versus 12 h after a power session, with strength not fully recovered at 48 h.
    https://peerj.com/articles/10044

## Grades

| Conclusion | Grade |
|---|---|
| A true 1RM is reliable (ICC ~0.97, CV ~4%) | W |
| First-ever 1RMs under-read in inexperienced lifters; the retest is higher (median ~5.5 kg lower body; up to ~11% over four sessions) | I |
| Rep-based estimates are accurate to a few kg for the squat from a 3–5RM in trained lifters | I |
| Equations under-predict the deadlift (9–14%) | I (one study, untrained, ~10RM-type loads) |
| Prediction accuracy falls as reps rise; stay ≤10, ideally 3–6 | W |
| RIR self-ratings are out by about 1 rep on average and better near failure | W (one meta-analysis, high heterogeneity) |
| Velocity-based estimates overestimate by ~4–5 kg; the minimum-velocity method is poor for the free squat | W |
| 1RM testing has a low injury rate with proper protocol; risk is higher in inexperienced lifters | I |
| A 3–5-attempt NSCA-style progression with 2–5 min rest | H (practitioner convention) |
| Heavy lower-body lifting blunts running for 24–48 h | I (small n, mostly untrained subjects) |

## Gaps and caveats

- No study found addresses 1RM testing in people with a history of Achilles tendinopathy. Any link to Achilles risk is unstudied: neither for nor against.
- Deadlift evidence is thin. The Grgic review has no deadlift studies, and LeSuer used untrained students. Neither the trap-bar nor the conventional deadlift has good-quality estimate-vs-measured data in what was found.
- DiStasio, LeSuer, Ritti-Dias, Halperin, Greig and Keogh were seen only via abstracts or secondary citations. Sample sizes and exact errors are not verified for these.
- NSCA protocol details came from secondary sources that disagree on rest (1–5 min) and increments. Check against the NSCA *Essentials* text before treating as fixed.
- Most fatigue studies used 6RM multi-set sessions, not a single-rep max test. A max test is lower volume, so its running cost may be smaller, but this is unmeasured.
- [inferred] Applied to the current working sets (arithmetic, not a test result): if 95 kg × 5 is near a true 5RM, Epley gives ~111 kg and Brzycki ~107 kg. If those sets have reps in reserve, both under-estimate. 100 kg × 5 on the deadlift gives ~117 kg (Epley) / ~113 kg (Brzycki), and the deadlift may be under-predicted further (finding 4).
- [inferred] Given findings 1–2, a first-ever 1RM is probably a conservative baseline, and a second test a few weeks later may read higher without any real strength gain. A 3RM, or an RIR-capped top set with an equation, trades a few kg of accuracy for lower technical demand. Either is a defensible choice. This is interpretation, not a source finding.

# Threshold and progression sessions for a recreational endurance runner

Question: what does the evidence say about one rotating weekly quality session (threshold reps in a long run, progression, cruise intervals, tempo) for a 30–40 km/week runner with a settled Achilles?

## Findings

1. **Polarised training gives a small VO2peak edge over other distributions, but no edge in race or time-trial performance.**
   Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca, 2024, *Sports Medicine*. Systematic review and meta-analysis, 17 studies (14 pooled), n = 437. Full text read.
   VO2peak SMD 0.24 (95% CI 0.01–0.48) favouring polarised; time trial SMD −0.01 (−0.28 to 0.25), no difference; no difference in threshold speed/power. The VO2peak advantage appeared only in highly trained athletes (SMD 0.46) and in interventions under 12 weeks; trained/developmental athletes and ≥12-week studies showed no difference. Authors note many studies reported intensity distribution poorly.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428

2. **An earlier, smaller meta-analysis favoured polarised over threshold-heavy training for time trials.**
   Rosenblat, Perrotta & Vicenzino, 2019, *J Strength Cond Res*. Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs, 4 studies (3 pooled). Abstract only.
   Time-trial ES −0.66 (95% CI −1.17 to −0.15) favouring polarised over a threshold model. Only three studies pooled; wide interval. Note the comparator was a *threshold-dominant* model, not a pyramidal one with a single threshold session.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863593/

3. **In recreational runners, polarised and between-thresholds training both improved 10 km; the difference was not significant.**
   Muñoz et al., 2014, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform*. RCT, n = 30 recreational runners, 10 weeks. Abstract only.
   10 km improved 5.0% (polarised) vs 3.6% (between-thresholds), ~41 s apart, not significant. A post hoc subset of runners who followed their prescription closely (6 vs 16) favoured polarised (ES 1.29, 90% CI 0.31–2.27). Small subset, post hoc.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23752040/

4. **A more threshold-heavy ("focused") distribution matched polarised gains in recreational runners on 17% less training time.**
   Festa et al., 2020, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*. RCT, n = 38 recreational runners, 8 weeks, TRIMP-matched. Abstract/summary only.
   Polarised ~77/3/20% (zones 1/2/3) vs focused ~40/50/10%; similar improvements; 29.9 h vs 24.8 h total.
   https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2019.00070

5. **In well-trained runners, pyramidal then polarised gave the best result, but every distribution worked when load was matched.**
   Filipas et al., 2021 (vol. 32, 2022 issue), *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. RCT, n = 60 well-trained men, 16 weeks, four arms. Abstract/record only.
   All groups improved; pyramidal→polarised gave the largest gains (~1.5% in 5 km time trial). Relative VO2peak was the only physiological correlate.
   https://www.pubmed.gov/34792817/

6. **The strongest single trial for polarised is in well-trained athletes and short; threshold-only training added little beyond economy.**
   Stöggl & Sperlich, 2014, *Frontiers in Physiology*. RCT, n = 48 well-trained endurance athletes, 9 weeks, four groups. Full text open access; read via summary.
   Polarised: VO2peak +11.7%, time to exhaustion +17.4%. Threshold group: no further gains apart from slight economy improvements. Note the "threshold" arm was threshold-heavy, not one session a week.
   https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2014.00033/full

7. **The polarised-is-optimal claim is contested by leading physiologists.**
   Burnley, Bearden & Jones, 2022, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. Debate/opinion piece ("Polarized training is not optimal for endurance athletes"). Record only. Opinion, not data.
   https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4076012?lng=en

8. **The Norwegian lactate-guided threshold model is a conceptual proposal built on elite practice, not a tested intervention.**
   Casado, Foster, Bakken & Tjelta, 2023, *IJERPH*. Narrative review. Abstract and summaries.
   Threshold intervals paced by lactate target ~2–4.5 mmol/L, checked every 1–3 reps; interval format lets runners hold higher speed at moderate metabolic cost. Elite users run 2–4 threshold sessions a week inside 150–180 km. Authors call for controlled trials.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10000870/

9. **In elite runners, volume of easy running and tempo/short-interval practice predicted performance; tempo grew in importance over years.**
   Casado, Hanley, Santos-Concejero & Ruiz-Pérez, 2021, *J Strength Cond Res*. Retrospective correlational, n = 85 Kenyan and Spanish elites, 7 years. Abstract only.
   Total distance r ≥ 0.75; easy runs r ≥ 0.68; tempo r ≥ 0.50; short intervals r ≥ 0.53. Correlational, elite.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31045681/

10. **"Norwegian singles" (2–3 sub-threshold sessions a week, all else easy) is a crowdsourced recreational adaptation with no trial evidence.** Practitioner.
    Marathon Handbook and Outside coverage; sub-threshold put at ~80–87% HRmax. Sources agree there is no proof it beats other sensible plans or prevents injury.
    https://marathonhandbook.com/norwegian-singles-training/
    https://run.outsideonline.com/training/norwegian-method-for-everyone?scope=anon

11. **No study was found that tests progression runs as a session type.**
    Searches found coaching sources only. Practitioner definitions: start easy, finish fast; variants include a short fast finish (Fitzgerald: last 1.5–5 km at half-marathon pace or quicker) and longer marathon-pace finishes (Pfitzinger, last 5–6 km near marathon pace; secondary sources disagree on exact wording). Pfitzinger reportedly warns against pushing fast finishes on good days. Practitioner.
    https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/?p=110
    https://www.active.com/running/articles/3-progression-runs-to-reach-race-day-success
    https://www.irunfar.com/your-ultra-training-bag-of-tricks-taking-progression-workouts-to-the-trails

12. **Practitioner dosing for threshold: ~20 min continuous tempo, cruise intervals of 5–15 min with ~1 min rest per 5 min, total ≈ 10% of weekly volume.** Practitioner (Daniels, via VDOT pages and secondary sources; book not read).
    One quoted version caps it at the lesser of 10% of weekly mileage or 60 min. At 30–40 km/week, 10% is 3–4 km, i.e. ~13–18 min at ~4:30/km.
    https://vdoto2.com/LearnMore/TrainingDefinitions
    https://lukehumphreyrunning.com/threshold-volume/

13. **The talk test tracks ventilatory thresholds well enough to set intensity without a lab.**
    Foster et al., 2018, *Kinesiology*. Review of the group's own studies. Abstract/summary only.
    Comfortable speech ("yes") ≈ below first ventilatory threshold; "yes, but…" ≈ around it; can't speak comfortably ≈ above. Evidence largely from one research group.
    https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/192696

14. **Critical speed marks the steady-state boundary and can be estimated from training/race data in recreational runners.**
    Smyth & Muniz-Pumares, 2020, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. Observational, recreational marathoners' raw training data. Read via summary.
    Recreational marathoners raced at ~85% of CS on average (~93% for 2:30 runners, ~79% for 6 h). Nixon, Kranen, Vanhatalo & Jones (MSSE) found CS matched maximal metabolic steady state better than MLSS, which sits slightly below it.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7664951

15. **Varying interval prescriptions within a block made no difference versus ordered progression when load was matched.**
    Sylta et al., 2016, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. RCT, n = 63 trained cyclists, 12 weeks, 24 interval sessions. Abstract/record only.
    Increasing (4×16 → 4×8 → 4×4 min), decreasing and mixed orders all improved 5–10% (40-min power, peak power, VO2peak); no between-group differences.
    https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2444540

16. **High training monotony and strain (load × monotony) were associated with illness.**
    Foster, 1998, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. Observational, n = 25 experienced athletes. Abstract only.
    Most illnesses occurred when individuals exceeded their own strain thresholds. Monotony here means day-to-day load variation, not exercise choice.
    https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4001707?lng=en

17. **Achilles tendon peak force and loading rate rise with running speed.**
    Starbuck et al., 2021, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. Lab biomechanics, n = 20 high-performing runners, 3.3–5.6 m/s. Abstract only.
    From slowest to fastest speed, peak Achilles force +19.5% and loading rate +57.3%, linked to shorter ground contact.
    https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1341565/the-effect-of-speed-on-achilles-tendon-forces-and-patellofemoral-joint-stresses-in-high-performing-endurance-runners

18. **Continuing running under a ≤5/10 pain-monitoring rule did no harm during Achilles rehab.**
    Silbernagel et al., 2007, *Am J Sports Med*. RCT, n = 38. Abstract/summary only.
    Continued loading (pain ≤5/10) vs 6 weeks' rest: no difference in outcomes; no negative effects found.
    https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389

## Grades

| Conclusion | Grade |
|---|---|
| Most training should be easy; a polarised or pyramidal distribution both work. Neither clearly wins on race performance | W (1, 3, 5) |
| Polarised gives a small VO2peak edge, mainly in highly trained athletes and short studies; little reason to expect it at 30–40 km/week | I (1) |
| One weekly threshold-type session inside mostly easy running is a reasonable, well-tolerated choice for recreational runners | I (3, 4, 9); dose specifics H |
| Norwegian double threshold and singles improve recreational runners | H (8, 10) – no trials |
| Threshold dose ≈ 20 min continuous or ≈10% of weekly volume, cruise reps 5–15 min with short rests | H (12) |
| Progression from ~20 to ~40 min at threshold over a block | H – no source tested a rate; practitioner convention only |
| Progression runs have specific benefits | H (11) – no trials found |
| Talk test and race-derived CS/threshold pace are adequate field substitutes for lab testing | I (13, 14) |
| Rotating session types is neither better nor worse physiologically when load is matched | I (15) – cyclists, interval types |
| Low day-to-day variation in load (monotony) is linked to illness | I (16) |
| Faster running loads the Achilles more per step than slower running | W for the mechanism (17); effect on injury risk H |
| Running within a ≤5/10 pain rule is compatible with tendon recovery | I (18) |
| Threshold reps inside a long run: fatigue/injury trade-off | H – practitioner reasoning only; no study found |

## Gaps and caveats

- Almost no trials use the setup in question: one threshold session a week at 30–40 km/week. Most comparisons put threshold-*dominant* training against polarised, which is not the same thing.
- No study of progression runs or threshold-in-long-run sessions was found. Advice there is coaching convention.
- No source gave an evidence-based rate for building threshold time (e.g. 20 → 40 min). Rule 5 (one variable at a time) is as good a guide as anything found.
- Most findings come from abstracts or summaries; numbers should be checked against full texts before being treated as exact.
- Practitioner pace equivalences (threshold ≈ 1-hour race pace, ≈ 5k pace + 15–20 s/km) were not checked against research here. On a 21:28 5k (4:18/km) a Daniels-type table puts threshold near 4:30/km `[inferred]`; a 3×10 min at 4:20–4:30 feeling conversational suggests either fitness ahead of that badly paced 5k, or the reps sat below threshold. A talk test or HR check on the next session would settle it.
- Starbuck's Achilles data are per step at sprint-to-tempo speeds in high-performing runners; no source quantified load per kilometre, or for threshold reps late in a long run when fatigued. One unconfirmed study suggested peak Achilles force falls as fatigue builds at fixed high intensity, so the fatigue effect is not simple.
- Variation evidence (Sylta) is in cyclists and covers interval length, not session types like progression vs tempo. The case for rotation rests mainly on enjoyment and adherence (rule 1), not physiology.
- Not medical advice. Achilles guidance here reflects the rehab literature found, not an individual assessment.

# Evidence: the Norwegian Method and double threshold training

Compiled from `raw/research/norwegian-method/` — four Marius Bakken articles (2000, 2022,
2025, 2026) and the community-maintained *Norwegian Singles* guide (PDF).
Grades: **W** well-supported · **I** reasonable inference · **H** practitioner heuristic.

**Sourcing caveat — read this before the rest.** This is not the same kind of source as the
other two evidence pages. Those were AI research passes over named academic literature, with
weak but real citation trails. This is overwhelmingly **one person's 25-year first-person
account of his own training and coaching**, written in "I believe" / "I suspect" / "in my
experience" throughout — which Bakken himself is honest about ("this is by no means a
scientific article"). Two genuinely checkable citations appear inside it:

- **Kjøsen Talsnes et al. 2024**, *Frontiers in Physiology* — a real, peer-reviewed, small
  (n=14) crossover trial. This is the one finding here that clears the same bar as the other
  evidence pages. **W**
- **Casado & Bakken 2023** — a review Bakken co-authored (PubMed 36900796), on why LT2 training
  works. Real and checkable, but a review, and Bakken is a co-author summarising his own
  position. **I**

Everything else — the muscle-tone model, the "Golden Zone" of 2.3–3.0 mmol/L, the specific
protocols attributed to Kristensen, Radcliffe's coach, or the Ingebrigtsens — is anecdote,
however detailed and however many numbers it carries.

**The Ingebrigtsen dispute.** Bakken's proof-of-success chain runs mainly through the
Ingebrigtsen family. In the 2025 article he quotes Gjert Ingebrigtsen's sworn court testimony:
*"This system is mine and only mine... no one has influenced it, internally or externally."*
That is a direct denial of Bakken's central claim, made under oath, in the middle of a family
legal dispute. Bakken disputes it back with his own correspondence excerpts. Neither is
independently verified here. Read every "and that's why the Ingebrigtsens are so good" line
as contested, not settled.

**Population mismatch.** The elite examples run 150–220 km/week: Bakken himself, Kristensen,
the Ingebrigtsens, Radcliffe. Jack peaks 60–70 km/week and is currently rebuilding from
15–19 km/wk. Even the Norwegian Singles guide — built specifically to scale this down for
recreational runners — treats double threshold as an advanced structure most readers should
never need. Nothing here should be read as "do what the Ingebrigtsens do, smaller."

## What actually applies to Jack's situation

### The core mechanism reframes the grey-zone problem usefully

Bakken's "sweet spot" — sub-threshold effort, controlled, run as **repeats with jog
recoveries**, never continuous unstructured running — sits at a pace that would often get
called grey-zone in Western coaching. The difference isn't the pace band. It's that it's
**dosed, bounded, and a quality session**, not an unfocused steady run at ambiguous effort.
The Norwegian Singles guide makes this explicit: a repetition finishes with "enough left for
another," effort checked by breath and a talk test, never chasing a heart-rate number, and the
work stops the moment control is lost. **I**

This is a genuinely useful answer to a gap `observations.md` already names: quality sessions
stopped after Florence and the pace drifted into an undefined middle. The fix these sources
propose isn't "run harder" or "run slower" — it's "make the moderate-pace running **structured**
again": short controlled reps with recovery jogs, at an effort you could repeat, rather than a
continuous run at a pace that's neither clearly easy nor clearly a session.

### The Singles progression method matches Phase A almost exactly

Norwegian Singles' onboarding rule — change **one existing run** into one controlled workout,
hold total time flat, don't add a second workout until repeated weeks show full recovery — is
close to what `plan-training` and Phase A are already doing by instinct, but with clearer
checks: finish able to do another rep; usual easy runs still feel easy; soreness isn't
accumulating; sleep and energy are normal. That's a concrete, testable version of "one variable
at a time" (§4.5) applied specifically to reintroducing quality work. **I**

Its "adjust your week" chapter (missed sessions, heavy legs, less time, illness) also matches
the model's existing rules closely enough to be reassuring rather than novel — restraint via
structure, not willpower, is the same idea `training-model.md` §3 already has, arrived at
independently.

### Double threshold — not a fit, and the sources say so themselves

Every population it's built for (Bakken's own racing at 5k/1500m, the Ingebrigtsens, Kristensen,
Casado, Radcliffe's marathon) is a competitive middle-to-long-distance athlete at high weekly
volume. Two things worth knowing, from Bakken's own words:

- *"This is only a point for the 1500m up to the 10k. You definitely need more continuous work
  for the marathon."* — Bakken, 2022. Even the method's originator excludes the marathon from
  its tightest form.
- Norwegian Singles, on ultras directly: *"The standard singles week does not cover all of
  those demands... adding double-threshold days is not a default solution."*

Jack's two targets — marathon and a 24-hour backyard ultra — are exactly the two events these
sources say the method doesn't reach. Combined with the volume mismatch and a tendon that
flared four months ago, there's no version of double threshold that fits here. **I**

## Supporting detail, lower stakes

### The NTNU split-session study

Kjøsen Talsnes et al. 2024, n=14 well-trained endurance athletes (VO2max ~69): one 6×10min
threshold session vs. the same volume split 3×10min morning / 3×10min evening. The split
version showed lower heart rate, lower lactate and lower RPE in the second bout than the first
— the opposite of the fatigue drift seen in the single long session — plus better next-day
recovery scores. **W**, but scoped to splitting one day's *volume* in half, not to running two
independent sessions. At Jack's current threshold dose (one session, ~30–40min), there's
nothing to split. Filed for completeness, not near-term relevance.

### Muscle tone and same-day recovery

Bakken's explanation for why a second same-day threshold session can feel *easier* than the
first — lower resting muscle tone, delayed-onset soreness not yet online, priming rather than
fatigue — is a coherent physiological story, but it's his own framework, unpublished beyond a
small pilot in his own master's thesis. **H**. Interesting reading, not something to build a
prescription on.

### Corroboration, not new information

Jones 2006's Radcliffe data (VO2max stable ~70 ml/kg/min across a decade; her gains came from
running economy and threshold speed, not the aerobic ceiling) is a real, well-documented paper
and matches what's already in `evidence-endurance-structure.md` and Jack's own 3:24→3:11 shift.
Doesn't change anything; reinforces it. **W**
